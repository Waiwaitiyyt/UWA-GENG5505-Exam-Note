### Question 1 — Explain the 4 stages of the project life cycle and why each is important

**The 4 stages** (per Hartley 2018, lectured in WK1b):
**Concept → Planning → Execution (monitoring & controlling) → Finalisation (and evaluation).**

#### Stage 1 — Concept Stage *(also called initiation, feasibility, or Stage 1)*

The **idea stage** in which the project is conceived. The team discusses preliminary goals, deliverables and strategic vision alignment; raises problems; conducts impact assessment in terms of **TBL and life cycle**; identifies potential benefits; researches alternative approaches; and determines provisional costing.

**Why it is important.** One of the most common causes of project failure is miscommunication, misunderstanding, and lack of clarity between stakeholder expectations and the project's final output. Stakeholders often ignore the basic principle of project management — *identify the problem before you try to solve it.* The concept stage enforces this discipline because it requires the project to be correctly **justified, investigated, assessed and approved**. It captures, documents and clarifies relevant information on the **four input constraints** found in every project: **Time, Cost, Resources and Specification** (Hartley 2018, WK1b).

*Example (PCH).* Initial purpose: replace the old rundown children's hospital with a new tertiary one; preliminary economic, social and environmental issues raised; impact assessment performed; budget approximated at 1.2 billion; site set in Nedlands; ~290 beds; bright child-friendly design.

#### Stage 2 — Planning Stage *(also called schedule, preparation, or Stage 2)*

Following the decision to proceed in Stage 1, all work required for successful completion is planned and scheduled. **Objectives are finalised, resources are assigned, quality is signed off (including TBL and life-cycle guidelines & standards), final costs are approved, timing is agreed, and all other administrative matters are determined** (WK1b).

**Why it is important.** Without a detailed plan, execution has no direction, resource alignment, or budget control. The planning stage prevents costly surprises and is where stakeholder alignment is consolidated — misalignment caught here is far cheaper to fix than misalignment caught during construction.

*Example (PCH).* Design detailed further; budget $1.2B funded by WA government; resources allocated; timeline outlining each construction phase, projected opening June 2015.

#### Stage 3 — Execution Stage *(also called implementation, control, or Stage 3)*

The project has commenced. Emphasis shifts to **tracking actual progress against the schedules developed in Stage 2 as the comparison point of reference**. All work (including TBL & life-cycle thinking) is **monitored, controlled and corrected** where necessary; schedules are reviewed, revised and updated as required (WK1b).

**Why it is important.** This is where the majority of project expenditure occurs; poor execution directly produces cost over-runs, delays and quality failures. Monitoring & control mechanisms allow early detection and correction of deviations before they compound.

*Example (PCH).* Physical construction commenced; delays occurred due to asbestos and water contamination; timelines repeatedly reviewed, revised and updated.

#### Stage 4 — Finalisation (and Evaluation) Stage *(also called completion, termination, hand-over, or Stage 4)*

The project has been completed and the deliverable handed over to the client. **Resources are disposed of or reassigned, the project is evaluated, reports are written and presented, and the administrative arm of the project is closed** (WK1b).

**Why it is important.** Project success cannot be measured solely against technical scope; the finalisation stage formally evaluates whether the project achieved its strategic purpose and stakeholder value. Lessons learned are documented so knowledge is retained for future projects. Formal hand-over establishes clear accountability between contractor and client.

*Example (PCH).* Formal acceptance & hand-over of the new PCH from John Holland to the WA Government; numerous government and consulting evaluation reports published.

The lecture (WK1b, "Project Management Lifecycle" slide) uses the precise wording **"Finalisation (and evaluation) stage — also called completion, termination, hand-over, or Stage 4"**, with deliverables being **handed over, resources disposed of or reassigned, the project evaluated, reports written and presented, and the administration arm of the project closed.** Added the four input constraints (Time, Cost, Resources, Specification) which the original answer omitted.

### Question 2 — Explain "sustainable approach", how it is consistent with TBL, and how applying TBL to every stage improves the project

#### A "sustainable approach", as defined in lecture (WK1a, Robbins et al. 2009)

> "A company's ability to achieve its business goals and increase **long-term shareholder value** by integrating economic, environmental and social opportunities into its business strategies — the **Triple Bottom Line (TBL)** approach."

And, more fundamentally:

> "Sustainability — **meeting the needs of people today without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.**"

The key insight from the slides: the long-term financial interests of a company are **not** mutually exclusive with acting fairly in the interests of stakeholders.

#### Indicators of sustainability — consistent with TBL (Labuschagne et al. 2005, WK1a)

| Dimension | Indicator examples | Guiding question |
|---|---|---|
| **Economic / Prosperity** | Financial health, economic performance, ROI, ROA, risk | Will the project generate prosperity & enhance the affected economies? |
| **Environmental / Planet** | Mineral & energy resources, air, water & land resources, emissions, waste, hazardous materials | Will the project cause long-term damage to the environment? |
| **Social / People** | Internal HR, external population, community, stakeholder participation | Will the project be implemented in a socially responsible manner & benefit the affected communities fairly & equitably? |

#### TBL applied across the 4 stages

**Concept** — Embed sustainability at the very point the idea is justified, so that the project is approved on TBL-aligned grounds (economic catalyst + social benefit + environmental constraints identified). PCH example: localising procurement to John Holland; tailoring spaces for children (social); $1.2B economic injection (economic); water-recycling and LED strategies (environmental).

**Planning** — TBL is built into risk identification, scope, contracts, quality standards and approval gates. Design choices in PCH such as the double-skinned façade (long-run energy saving) and bright play areas (social) were locked in as planning decisions.

**Execution** — TBL becomes an active management tool: studies confirm no significant environmental impact; sustainable choices conserve labour, natural resources and energy; the resulting IP and reputation produce long-term returns for stakeholders.

**Finalisation** — Project success cannot be measured by scope delivery alone; further analysis using TBL is required. **Quality checklists** are useful tools to ensure TBL is considered during quality analysis. A company giving regular attention to sustainability earns a strong positive public image (e.g. PCH's community engagement and outreach to vulnerable populations / low-SES communities).

#### Benefits of life cycle thinking (WK1b)
- Savings in labour, resources & energy
- Positive image → competitive advantage
- Intellectual property & long-term returns
- Alignment with TBL

### B1 — Differentiate the 3 numerical project-selection methods; which is the most exhaustive?

The lecture (**WK2a, "Project selection: Numeric models"**) lists three numeric methods:

1. **Payback Period** — the time required to recover the initial investment.
   Formula: `Payback = Cost of project ÷ Annual cash revenue`.
   Simple, but ignores cash flow after the payback point and the time value of money.

2. **Return on Investment (ROI)** — the overall profit on an investment expressed as a percentage of total investment.
   Formula: `ROI = (Net profit ÷ Total investment) × 100`.
   Example from the slide: net profit $40,000 over 6 years on an investment of $100,000 → ROI = 40%.
   Considers the entire cash-flow period, **but does not account for the time value of money**.

3. **Net Present Value (NPV) — the most exhaustive** — the projected profitability of an investment based on **future (anticipated) cash-flows discounted (from year 2) at a stated interest rate**.
   `NPV = Σ (Cash flow × Discount factor)`, where `Discount factor = 1 / (1 + i)ⁿ`.
   Most exhaustive because it accounts for both the entire cash-flow period **and** the time value of money.

> ⚠ **Correction:** Original answer's ROI formula and Payback formula were correct but missing context. Added the key distinction that **ROI ignores time value of money** but covers the whole cash-flow period — which is why NPV is the most exhaustive. NPV formula clarified per WK2a slide.

### B2 — Stages of team development (Tuckman, lectured in WK5a)

Team development is **not a linear process** — teams can regress to earlier stages. The five stages are:

1. **Forming** — Everyone comes together for the first time; introductions, attempts to "break the ice"; tentative relationships; little trust; unknown goals; ambiguity in goal and role; hidden agendas; "cards held close to the chest." (*Group example: first class meeting.*)

2. **Storming** — Inevitable and natural conflict appears. Caused by personality clashes, team-role disputes, leadership issues, or any other aspect of the team's processes. (*Meetings 2–3, no clear direction on project progress.*)

3. **Norming** — The "unwritten" rules, codes of conduct and acceptable behaviours (norms) are developed, shared and agreed. Once established, all members know what to expect from each other. (*Regular meetings with updates.*)

4. **Performing** — Team members are ready to perform their assigned work in a united, cohesive and productive manner. (*Group actively contributing to the report.*)

5. **Adjourning** — The project has finished and the team members disband. (*Project submitted, final presentation completed.*)

> ⚠ **Correction:** Original answer spelled stage 4 as **"Preforming"** — should be **"Performing"**. Also added that Norming involves the development of **"unwritten rules" / norms** specifically (per WK5a slide).

### B4 — Important considerations for good communication; choose 2 stakeholders and discuss barriers

**Considerations (per WK5b, "Planning communications management" and "Managing project communications"):**

- Communication is essential to project success — 75–90% of project time is spent communicating (writing, reading, talking, listening).
- Information should be **clear, concise, accurate, timely, targeted, appropriate and complete**, and provided **in the right format, at the right time, to the right audience, with the right impact**.
- Distinguish four types of input: **Fact** (indisputable truth), **Fantasy** (someone's opinion/interpretation), **Folklore** (rumour, gossip, hearsay), **Feelings** (intuition, ego, emotion).
- The **Communication Process Model** (Belch & Belch 2004) describes communication as the passing of information between **sender** and **receiver** through encoding, message, channel, decoding — with **noise** capable of disrupting any step.

**Example: Project Manager (sender) → Contractor (receiver).**
- The contractor may **decode incorrectly** — different attitudes, experiences and beliefs produce different "cognitive views" of the message.
- The scope may have changed and the contractor has **not been informed**.

**Example: Project Manager → Sponsor.**
- The sponsor may receive **filtered or incomplete reports**, leading to misaligned expectations on cost, schedule or scope.

**Common communication barriers (WK5b):**
- Lack of client involvement
- Poorly informed stakeholders
- Lack of meetings, or too many meetings with no action
- Lack of reporting requirements
- Poor and incomplete documentation
- Frequent scope change
- Changing project personnel
- Lack of auditing the project to identify lessons learned

### B5 — Why stakeholder management is important + 4 (actually 5) stakeholder strategies

**Why important** (WK2b):
- Stakeholders affect the project's outcome — they can support or hinder it.
- They cannot be ignored — they **will** impact ultimate success.
- They must be identified prior to commencement and throughout the project.
- Their information needs must be managed throughout the project lifecycle.
- They bring diversity of ideas, contributions, support and challenges.
- Poor stakeholder management risks projects going over time, over budget, and failing to meet targets.
- Good stakeholder management also benefits future business — recommendations and re-hire opportunities.

**Five response strategies to stakeholder pressures in global projects (Aaltonen et al. 2009, WK2b):**

1. **Adaptation strategy** — Obeying the demands and rules presented by stakeholders; adjusting to stakeholder pressures so the planned objectives can still be achieved.
2. **Compromising strategy** — Negotiating with stakeholders; opening dialogue, listening to their requests, and offering compensations where appropriate (**caveat: question the ethical implications**).
3. **Avoidance strategy** — Loosening attachment to stakeholders and their claims; transferring responsibility for dealing with the claims to others in the project network (**caveat: is this ethical?**).
4. **Dismissal strategy** — Ignoring stakeholder demands — i.e. not considering stakeholder requirements when implementing the project stages.
5. **Influence strategy** — Dealing proactively with the demands from stakeholders; creating and communicating value to stakeholders while building relationship with them.

Also corrected the typo *"loosing attachment"* → "loosening attachment", and added that Adaptation = "obey & adjust" while Influence = "deal proactively + create value" (the original answer's wording was slightly off).

### B6 — Life cycle thinking and why it is important to project management

**Life cycle thinking (LCT)** is a strategy that takes into account *all the impacts (economic, social and environmental — i.e. TBL) that a product or service will have throughout its life cycle.* It enables project managers to see the lasting impact of their decisions on TBL.

**The "cradle-to-grave" life cycle** (Mont et al. 2007, lectured in WK1b) covers:
- Raw materials → Resource extraction
- Manufacture → Production
- Distribution
- Usage / Consumption
- Waste disposal (and any resulting pollution)

**Project management** (Hartley 2018, WK1a) — "the application of knowledge, skills, tools and techniques to project activities to meet project requirements. Achieved through the application and integration of the project management processes of **initiating, planning, executing, monitoring & controlling, and closing**." (Note: PMBOK uses 5 process groups; Hartley collapses these to the 4 lifecycle stages.)

**Life cycle impact assessment:** *an impact is a positive or negative result of an effect of a product, process and/or activity, including all social, economic and environmental consequences and implications.*

**Why it matters:**
- It is consistent with TBL and forces project managers to consider the *whole* product life — not just construction.
- It enables long-term decision-making and identifies hidden environmental or social costs that traditional financial analysis misses.
- It builds positive public image, intellectual property and long-term returns.

Also clarified the PMBOK 5 process groups (initiating, planning, executing, monitoring & controlling, **closing**) — the original answer omitted "monitoring & controlling" and "initiating".

### B7 — Stages of quality management (WK4b)

The lecture title is **"The road to quality"** — four stages:

1. **Quality Planning** — All mandated quality standards, operational definitions and business requirements relevant to the project are clearly identified and agreed, and ensured to be achievable and measurable throughout the project. **Quality is planned in from the start; not simply inspected in later.**

2. **Quality Assurance (QA)** — A declaration / guarantee that overall project performance (input, process & output) is evaluated on a regular basis to give all stakeholders the confidence that relevant quality standards will be satisfied. Tools include the quality management plan, regular meetings, scheduled/random quality audits, ISO standards (9000 for quality, 14000 for environmental management, 26000 for social responsibility).

3. **Quality Control (QC)** — Monitors specific task and project results to identify, measure and eliminate causes of unsatisfactory performance, while ensuring quality compliance is demonstrated and achieved. *Control implies measurement.* Tools: peer reviews, physical inspection, control charts, scatter diagrams, checklists, Pareto diagrams, statistical sampling, flowcharts, cause-and-effect (fishbone) diagrams, trend analysis.

4. **Quality (Continuous) Improvement** — A culture, commitment and ownership of *what* the project is delivering and *how well* it is being delivered. Innovation, risk-taking and efficiencies all support this. Tools: regular performance reporting, meetings/debriefs, decision gates, walkthroughs, scenario analysis, evaluation reports, suggestion boxes, user feedback.

> ⚠ **Correction:** Original answer was on point. Added the QA tools (ISO standards 9000/14000/26000) and QC tools (control charts, Pareto, fishbone, etc.) which are explicitly lectured. Also added the key phrase from WK4b: "**quality is planned in from the start, not randomly inspected in.**"

### B8 — PERT: definition, advantages and disadvantages (WK3b)

**PERT (Program Evaluation and Review Technique)** is a *statistical / network-analysis* tool used to analyse and represent the tasks involved in completing a project. It illustrates: project logic and how it is tied together; relationships between tasks; flow of work; the **critical path**; potential bottlenecks; how each task is required for project completion.

**The 3-point estimate** (PERT, Beta distribution, PMBOK 2013):
`Te = (To + 4Tm + Tp) / 6`
where To = optimistic, Tm = most probable, Tp = pessimistic time.
*Example from slide:* To=5, Tm=10, Tp=30 → Te = 12.5 days.

**Advantages** (WK3b — "PERT network diagram: Advantages & disadvantages"):
- Excellent visual & interactive graphic to demonstrate the schedule
- Participative decision-making
- Joint risk identification & response strategy
- Negotiated concessions
- Improved team ownership
- Shows the **critical path**
- Eliminates idle time

**Disadvantages:**
- Difficult to read if the project is large
- **No timeline** (unlike Gantt)
- Difficult to monitor & report performance
- Not always easy to understand
- Limited amount of information that can be displayed

> ⚠ **Correction:** Original answer was correct on most items but missed that PERT also includes **"negotiated concessions"** and **"eliminates idle time"** as advantages. The 3-point estimate formula was not in the original answer — added for completeness.

### B9 (first) — Balanced Scorecard (BSC) for projects (Stewart 2001, WK4b)

The BSC is a **performance measurement framework** that includes strategic performance metrics to give project managers and key stakeholders a more "balanced" view of project performance.

**Four perspectives:**

- **Customer perspective** — Customer satisfaction, economic value added, intended objectives.
- **Financial perspective** — Within budget; variance between original and final budget; project costs compared to industry standards.
- **Project / Internal perspective** — Team satisfaction, resource management.
- **Growth & Innovation perspective** — Best practices identified, ongoing improvement, innovative-ideas rate, **TBL, life cycle**.

**BSC across the 4 stages** (per slide):
- **Stage 1 (Concept)** — Initial measurement to establish a baseline for project planning.
- **Stage 2 (Planning)** — BSC benchmarks are included in the overall project plan.
- **Stage 3 (Execution)** — BSC measurements are implemented and initial benchmarks are used to compare and improve BSC outcomes.
- **Stage 4 (Finalisation)** — BSC measurements are reviewed and documented in the final report to support best practices and lessons learned.

### B9 (second, numbered as 9 in original — actually Q10) — Contracts: legally-binding requirements and 3 types (WK6b)

**A contract is a legally binding agreement between two or more parties to act, or refrain from acting, in a particular way — creating legal rights and obligations enforceable in law.**

**Essential elements (minimum 7) of a legally binding contract:**
1. **Offer** (capable of being accepted)
2. **Acceptance** (capable of being communicated)
3. **Intention / Consent** of each party to be legally bound (freely given)
4. **Consideration** (something is given back for the promise, e.g. money)
5. **Mutuality** (neither party is disadvantaged)
6. **Capacity** (both parties are not under any duress)
7. **Legality** (it is not an illegal activity)

Plus: inclusion of both express & implied terms, conditions & warranties, and provision for breach, remedy & other non-performance redress.

**Three types of contracts:**

| Type | Description | Customer risks | Contractor risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fixed-price** | Delivery of a well-defined product (goods/service) for a fixed price | At mercy of sole-source contractor; incomplete specs lead to contract changes; contractor may use cheaper materials | Requires careful cost & schedule estimation; cost growth → unprofitable project |
| **Cost-reimbursable** | Seller is paid for actual costs incurred plus a fee representing seller's profit | Final cost unknown; sole-source contractor; poor specification leads to more costs & contract changes | Fee percentage declines as costs rise; rising costs damage relationship with customer |
| **Time & Materials (T&M)** | Contains features of both fixed-fee and cost-plus contracts | No specification; open-ended; sole-source contractor | Fixed rates; no defined tasks |

> ⚠ **Correction:** Original answer listed **"offer, acceptance, consideration, capacity, legality, mutuality, consent"** (7 items). Lecture (WK6b) confirms 7 essential elements but the precise lecture wording uses **"Intention / Consent of each party to be legally bound (freely given)"** rather than just "consent". Also added that the lecture explicitly requires *inclusion of both express & implied terms, conditions & warranties, and provision for breach & remedy.*

### B10 — Budgeting techniques (WK4a)

Per WK4a, "Approaches to budgets":

- **Traditional** — Previous year's level of performance is the foundation for next year's figures.
- **Zero-based** — Ignores previous results; each activity and outlay is justified. Each activity starts with zero spending.
- **Program** — Activities are grouped together for projecting costs generated by each program or major activity.
- **Top-down** — Based on pooling the knowledge of senior managers and past results. Project costs are estimated and then passed to lower-level managers who continue the breakdown into further estimates.
- **Bottom-up** — Individual task budgets are estimated in detail by the people directly responsible for doing or managing the work. Estimates are aggregated to give the total project cost.

**Two perspectives on budgets** (WK4a):
- *Advantages*: improved decision-making; effective cost control; more reliable profits; assess operational financial requirements.
- *Disadvantages*: top-to-bottom support difficult to get; planning delays action; often "locked" and inflexible; restrictive of innovation & change.

### B12 — Why scope management is important + what a scope management plan should include (WK3a)

**A scope management plan documents** *"…how the project scope will be defined, validated and controlled"* (PMBOK 2013). It:
- Establishes the **direction and guidance parameters** on how the scope (project or product/service based) will be managed.
- Provides a **formal mechanism** to limit, assess and authorise changes on a consistent and transparent basis.

**Defining the scope includes:**
- What is required (**inclusions**) and what isn't required (**exclusions**)
- Establishes a **scope baseline** for comparisons and updates
- Forms the foundation of the project plan
- Investigates if **expectation meets capability** — *expectations don't always match capability*
- Identifies project deliverables, results and benefits

**Why important — Controlling the scope (WK3a):**
- **Scope will always change over time** ("scope creep"); other words for scope creep are innovation, continuous improvement, client change of mind, or just poor management.
- Scope control requires a **written process with formal approval**.
- Proposed changes should be assessed against **all other project variables** (time, cost, risk, contracts, quality, TBL) and approved by key stakeholders.
- Implemented changes must produce **updated project plans and related documents**.

**Causes of scope creep** (WK5b): poor initial requirements; unanswered questions on deliverables; lack of stakeholder involvement; evolving "exceeding expectations" mentality; discovery of new "solutions"; ineffective PM; external environmental factors.

> ⚠ **Correction:** Original answer was largely correct but missed the formal **scope-creep causes** taught in WK5b and the precise PMBOK definition.

### B15 — Information in the project close-out report (WK7)

The close-out (finalisation) report — and the activities surrounding it (WK7, "Closing down the project"):

## Section C — Ethics

### C1 — Utilitarianism, deontology and virtue-based ethics (WK9b)

The lecture identifies these as the three **main ethics theories**:

**1. Utilitarian / Consequences-based theory.**
*An action is judged ethical or unethical based on its consequences / outcome.*
- Founders: Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832) and John Stuart Mill (19ᵗʰ c.).
- Utilitarian thinking: *"the end justifies the means."*
- Forward-looking; an action is right if it produces the **greatest good for the greatest number** / maximises overall utility — the most common form of **consequentialism**.
- Basic principles: (a) maximise benefits, (b) minimise costs.
- Dominates Western business thinking — e.g. cost–benefit analysis.
- **Issues:** minority rights at risk; who decides what "good" is; what counts as a consequence; how far into the future should consequences be calculated.
- *Example from lecture:* a pharmaceutical company releases a drug with minor side effects; the drug benefits a large population more than the small number troubled by side effects.
- *Ford Pinto case* (1970) — Ford did a cost/benefit analysis and chose not to fix a fuel-tank design flaw because the cost ($137 M) exceeded the calculated benefit ($49.5 M). A classic utilitarian-thinking failure.

**2. Deontological / Duty-based theory.**
*Actions are judged ethical standing alone and without regard to consequences.*
- From Greek *deon* = duty.
- Most famous deontologist: **Immanuel Kant (1724–1804)**.
- Backward-looking; do the right thing simply because it is the right thing to do — **means rather than end**.
- Kant proposed three formulations of the **categorical imperative**:
  1. *Act as if, through your actions, you were making universal law for everyone to follow* — e.g. don't bribe contractors, don't mislead customers.
  2. *Always treat any human being (self included) as an end in themselves, never merely as a means to an end* — e.g. respect employee/customer safety and dignity.
  3. *Act as if you were a member of a community of fellow moral legislators who are ends in themselves* — e.g. do not force your will upon another.
- **Typical duties:** fidelity, gratitude, justice, beneficence, self-improvement, non-maleficence.
- *Example:* a manager turning down a promotion that requires forgery because it conflicts with his principles.

**3. Virtue-based theory.**
*The focus is on the individual, not on the action.*
- Ongoing quest for goodness and virtue — individuals should seek to live a virtuous life.
- A "good person" does the right thing.
- Roots in Aristotle's eudaimonia (flourishing): virtue lies in the **mean** between excess and deficiency; highest form of mortal activity is contemplation; perfection through moderation of reason and desire.

> ⚠ **Correction:** Original answer's coverage was correct in essence but used "*the man turning down the promotion which required him to commit forgery*" without citing Kant's three formulations. Also missed the **Ford Pinto case** as the lecture's flagship example of utilitarian failure. Added.

### C2 — 5 (actually 8) Key principles in the ethics code for engineers (WK10b)

The lecture (**WK10b, "Roles of codes of ethics for engineers"** — Schinzinger et al. 2010) lists **8** roles of codes of ethics for engineers:

2. **Guidance** — Codes provide helpful guidance by articulating the main obligations of engineers.
3. **Inspiration** — Codes express the profession's collective commitment to ethics, providing positive stimulus for ethical conduct.
4. **Shared standards** — Diversity of moral viewpoints among engineers makes it essential that the profession establishes explicit (minimum but high) standards. The public is assured of a standard of excellence; professionals get a fair playing field for clients.
5. **Support for responsible professionals** — A publicly proclaimed code allows an engineer under pressure to act unethically to say: *"I am bound by the code of ethics of my profession, which states that…"*
6. **Education and mutual understanding** — Codes encourage shared understanding among professionals, the public and government bodies about engineers' moral responsibilities.
7. **Deterrence and discipline** — Codes serve as the formal basis for investigating unethical conduct. Professional societies do suspend or expel members whose conduct is proven unethical.
8. **Contributing to the profession's image** — Codes present a positive image to the public of an ethically committed profession. Reputation is essential in sustaining public trust.

### C5 — 7 Stages of the Ethical Cycle (Van de Poel & Royakkers 2007, WK10b)

1. **Case** — the cycle starts with a particular case of moral complication.

2. **Moral problem statement** — *precise / specific formulation*. Clearly state what the problem is, for whom it is a problem, and the moral nature of the problem.

3. **Problem analysis** — *clear identification of relevant elements*:
   (a) stakeholders; (b) moral values involved; (c) relevant facts.

4. **Options for action** — *all possible options should be identified* — **thinking creatively is essential**.

5. **Ethical judgement** — *thorough application of moral concepts, ethical theories, models and frameworks* (utilitarian, deontological, virtue, code of ethics, etc.).

6. **Reflection** — *reflect on the outcome of the previous stage* — does the chosen action stand up to scrutiny?

7. **Morally accepted action(s)** — actions decided upon are presented and implemented; chosen to solve the case / ethical dilemma.

## Additional Lecture Content Worth Knowing (not in original docx)

The following major topics appear in lectures but are absent from the original `5505.docx`. Including them here for exam coverage.

### Project selection — non-numeric models (WK2a)
- **Sacred cow** — protected by senior executive.
- **Operating necessity** — to maintain operational functionality (e.g. during a crisis).
- **Competitive necessity** — to maintain competitive advantage / match competitors.
- **Product extension** — extending existing product lines.
- **Comparative benefit** — for companies seeking multiple projects with differing benefits.

### Risk management (WK6a)
**Risk** = the possibility of loss or injury; a situation with measured impact (positive or negative) on an outcome.
**Process** (iterative): Identify → Assess → Analyse → Manage → Monitor & Evaluate → Review.
**5×5 priority grid:** Probability × Impact = priority score (1–6 = existing controls; 8–12 = project manager intervention; 15–25 = senior management intervention).
**Possible risk responses:** Reject, Accept, Mitigate, Share, Transfer, Enhance (for opportunities), Exploit (for opportunities).
**Tools:** SWOT, PESTELG, risk registers, fishbone diagrams, lessons-learned reports, simulations, decision trees, sensitivity analysis, contingency planning, financial modelling.

### Cross-cultural business ethics (WK11b)
**6 universal moral values for corporate codes of ethics (Schwartz 2005):**
1. Trustworthiness (integrity, honesty, loyalty, transparency)
2. Respect (for human rights)
3. Responsibility (accountability, self-restraint)
4. Fairness (equity, impartiality)
5. Caring (no harm)
6. Citizenship (TBL, life-cycle thinking, obeying the law)

**UN Global Compact — 10 Principles** in human rights, labour, environment, anti-corruption.

**4 options for a multinational facing home-host norm conflict (Beauchamp et al. 2008):**
1. Follow norms of home country (patriotic).
2. Follow norms of host country ("when in Rome…").
3. Follow whichever norm is most profitable.
4. Follow whichever norm is morally best.

### Moral development — Stages hierarchy model (Linstead et al. 2004 / Kohlberg, WK11a)
- **Pre-conventional morality** (Stages 1–2) — self-interest, "what's in it for me?"
- **Conventional morality** (Stages 3–4) — pleasing close associates (3); professional integrity and lawful pursuit (4).
- **Post-conventional morality** (Stages 5–7) — concern for social justice and collective well-being (TBL approach); less than 1/5 of adults reach Stage 5, and Stages 6–7 are rare.

### Line-drawing approach to ethical dilemmas (Fledderman 2011, WK11a)
- Place a **positive paradigm (PP)** at one end (unambiguously morally acceptable) and a **negative paradigm (NP)** at the other end (unambiguously unacceptable).
- Place the problem **P** under consideration between them, along with hypothetical examples.
- The closer **P** is to the positive paradigm, the more morally acceptable the action.
- Caveat: subjective, legal, political and community-perception aspects must also be considered.

### Negotiation — Getting to Yes (Fisher & Ury 1991, WK6b)
- Separate the people from the problem.
- Focus on interests, not positions.
- Invent options for mutual gain.
- Insist on using objective criteria.
- Develop your **BATNA** (Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement) and be patient.

### Setting objectives — SMART (WK3a)
**S**pecific, **M**easurable, **A**chievable, **R**ealistic, **T**ime-framed.

### Cradle-to-Grave life cycle (WK1b, Mont et al. 2007)
Raw materials → Resource extraction → Manufacture/Production → Distribution → Usage/Consumption → Waste disposal.
